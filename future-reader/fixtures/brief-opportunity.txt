2. PINPOINT CRUCIAL TIMING MOMENTS

Define a moment as crucial when crossing it changes which actions are feasible, which outcomes remain reachable, what evidence can be obtained, or whether recovery is still possible. Require a named change, its consequence, its evidence, and the uncertainty around its timing. Rank moments by their effect on the stated objective and hard constraints.

Detect changes from deadlines, prerequisite arrivals, threshold crossings, state transitions, resource or permission changes, evidence expiration, synchronization changes, and the earliest or latest safe time to start, commit, stop, or recover. Support periodic windows, windows triggered by events or state, competing or overlapping windows, interruptions, and windows with an unknown opening or closing. Model how each candidate action could open, extend, shorten, or consume a window.

For each timing estimate, record the clock, frame, granularity, and source of uncertainty. Separate a timestamp observed directly, a deadline specified by policy, a boundary computed from a model, and an inferred boundary. Return a point only at the resolution the evidence supports. Return a range, competing ranges, or an unresolved boundary when appropriate. Where timing is relative, preserve event order or elapsed time and the anchor needed to interpret it. Never convert a coarse estimate into a precise countdown by formatting alone.

Compute distinct times for opening, latest start, commitment, last reversible action, closure, and verification where relevant. Account for sensing, synchronization, planning, approval, queueing, execution, and verification delays. For a hard deadline, use a justified bound or explicitly chosen risk tolerance. Include a recovery margin when policy or the operation requires it. If an action is theoretically available but cannot finish in time under its constraints, show the missed margin and any feasible fallback.

3. DEFINE WINDOW-OF-OPPORTUNITY STATE VECTORS

Use a typed, versioned representation of the state needed to make a timing decision. Reuse authoritative fields where available. Give every vector a stable identity and link it to the relevant record, transition, and causal dependencies.

Include, where applicable:

- Scope, domain, entity, objective, candidate action, outcome definition, and time horizon.
- Observation time, evidence cutoff, freshness, clock source, reference frame, units, and sequence.
- Current window state, opening and closing conditions, earliest start, latest viable start, commitment point, last reversible point, and time remaining as a value or range.
- Prerequisites, dependencies, hard constraints, permissions, available resources, and competing uses of the same resources.
- Estimated action duration and the latency budget for sensing, approval, execution, verification, and recovery.
- Conditional outcome probabilities or supported bounds, assumptions, model version, uncertainty, calibration evidence, and dependencies among outcomes.
- Expected benefits and costs where measurable, irreversible effects, downside, opportunity cost, feasible alternatives, and fallback conditions.
- The observation or threshold most likely to change the decision, its expected arrival time, and whether it can arrive before the opportunity closes.
- Provenance, actual outcome when available, reason for each state transition, and the last verified checkpoint.

Support states such as unresolved, upcoming, open, narrowing, committed, closed, missed, invalidated, and fulfilled. Derive valid transitions from the current domain contract. Define any reopening rule explicitly. Preserve unknown values and differentiate unknown, inapplicable, estimated, and verified fields. Window state and fulfillment state are separate. An open window establishes eligibility to consider an action. Fulfillment requires evidence that acceptance criteria were met.

Treat the vector as a decision record. Permit numeric vectorization for a specific model when its coordinate definitions and information loss are documented. Preserve units, meaning, evidence, and access scope across representations. Keep original domain semantics available for reconstruction.

4. ALIGN CHANCE WITH PROBABILITY ACROSS DOMAINS

Represent chance as the possibility of outcomes under stated conditions. Quantify it when evidence and a defensible probability model support quantification. Define every probability by outcome, action, conditions, reference population or model, and time horizon. Distinguish baseline probability, probability conditional on taking an action, probability of completing within the window, and probability of the desired outcome after timely completion. Estimate joint or conditional probabilities where needed. Multiply marginal probabilities only when the required independence assumptions are justified.

Distinguish inherent variability, measurement error, model uncertainty, incomplete knowledge, and sensitivity to changing conditions. Attach the basis for every estimate, such as an analytic model, empirical frequency, calibrated forecast, simulation, expert judgment, or declared prior. Report sample size, selection effects, correlation, and calibration where relevant. Use intervals, scenarios, or qualitative uncertainty when precision is unsupported. Preserve the difference between a simulation frequency and an observed frequency. For quantum domains, use the applicable quantum measurement model and preserve the meaning of the measurement basis and outcomes. Software alternatives, statistical mixtures, and physical quantum states require their own semantics.

Update estimates when fresh evidence arrives. Preserve prior estimates, evidence cutoff, model version, and what changed. Use held-out cases, rolling-origin validation, or equivalent time-aware evaluation. Track calibration and decision quality where outcomes are observable. Detect distribution drift, selection bias, data leakage, repeated looks at the same evidence, and censored windows whose outcomes cannot be observed. In replay, score a decision using what was available at the time. Preserve the observed outcome separately.

Compare act now, wait for evidence, prepare a reversible step, choose a different path, and let the opportunity pass where each is feasible. Estimate benefits, costs, risks, and opportunity costs with explicit assumptions. Use expected value when probabilities and consequences support it. Use ranges, sensitivity analysis, or robust choices when estimates are weak. Enforce hard constraints regardless of estimated benefit. Identify the threshold at which the recommendation changes. Keep low-probability, high-consequence cases visible when their consequences materially affect the decision.

Apply a shared interface across domains and verify each domain’s own rules for timing, probability, causality, and acceptance. Cover examples such as software recovery deadlines, research measurement windows, engineering or logistics handoffs, and quantum circuit measurement or coherence constraints where supported. A domain adapter must declare which fields it can compute, validate, or leave unresolved. Verify at least two substantially different domains through the same contract. Label simulated and hardware-verified capabilities explicitly.

Reversible oracle:

- Inspect a prior state using the evidence and permissions applicable at the selected cutoff.
- Preview a new branch, verified inverse, checkpoint restore, replay, or compensation where available.
- Show which downstream decisions relied on the prior state and which external consequences persist.
- Recalculate opportunity windows using the current clock and dependencies. A restored record can preserve a prior opportunity for inspection. Re-execution requires current eligibility.
- Respect limits from measurement, reset, noise, and discarded information in quantum circuits.

Future oracle:

- Translate problems into objectives, hard constraints, acceptance conditions, and budgets.
- Compare feasible paths and circuits. Show when to act, when waiting is valuable, and when waiting consumes the remaining margin.
- Show what changes the recommendation, including clock uncertainty, completion-time uncertainty, probability thresholds, and the next decisive evidence.

- Report the best candidate evaluated within scope and budget. Claim global optimality only when established.
- Compile circuits against target qubit count, connectivity, gate set, measurement, reset, and classical control. Compare with classical baselines and report compiled depth, resources, and observed outcomes.

12. CERN VERIFICATION FIXTURE

Preserve the evidence classes and caveats below. Verify source references before incorporating published facts.

- CERN announced the shutdown schedule in 2024. Final beams circulated June 27, 2026. Long Shutdown 3 began June 29, 2026. Beams are planned for June 2030. Preserve announcement, event, and plan dates.
- CMS 99.6% referred to photon sample purity. The 1968 Fermilab design report used 99.6% of c for injected protons. The original source of the modern photon-speed claim remains unverified.
- The 1964 CERN gamma-ray result was 299,770 ± 40 km/s over 31.450 ± 0.0015 m with air, beryllium, and Mylar reported. Keep distinct from modern LHC conditions. CERN’s 1977 muon storage ring tested time dilation. OPERA’s early arrival was traced to timing equipment and the dedicated follow-up was consistent with zero. Glasgow and Heriot-Watt measured structured-light axial group delay.
- BIPM Rapid UTC analysis covered 59 labs, June 8–July 12, 2026. Median daily offset changes for June 27–30 were −0.1, +0.1, and −0.3 ns. Swiss-to-overseas comparisons showed no abrupt shift at daily resolution. Circular T was a five-day cross-check. BIPM fractional estimates of the TAI scale interval relative to SI were (−0.30 ± 0.09) × 10^-15 and (−0.09 ± 0.12) × 10^-15 for May 29–June 28 and June 28–July 28. Preserve the limits for short, local, and common-mode effects. Clock differences alone do not resolve photon speed.
- LHC model inputs were a 14.3 m dipole, 2,803.95 m radius, and 44.04 × 34.28 mm opening. About 26.7 km is the ring circumference. The model ends at first wall hit. Gaps of 0–2 m, sampled launch angles, and offsets were assumptions. Distinguish vacuum flight-time reference from actual sector pressure and temperature during operation or shutdown.
- The 100,000 cases used 100 batches of 1,000, stratified by emission position and horizontal angle. Hit counts were 22,253 emitting bend, 6,603 straight gap, and 71,144 next bend. Median 11.42 m yields 38.091 ns at c and 38.244 ns at hypothetical 0.996c. Extra delay is about 153 ps, with 142–169 ps for the middle 90%. An extra 4.6 cm at c gives the same median reading. These are simulation outputs.
- The direct solver and 45-step bisection reference agreed on classifications with less than 0.4 pm maximum numerical path difference. The trajectory-only median benchmark across 11 runs was 66.7 ms to 3.06 ms, about 22× on that environment. Rebenchmark and distinguish numerical agreement from physical model error.
- In 10,000 synthetic trials per scenario at 5, 10, and 20 m with 3 ps random uncertainty per averaged baseline, a fixed electronics offset was distinguishable from distance-dependent delay. A proportional path error and 0.996c both produced about 13.4 ps/m. A 1 cm path error adds about 33 ps. The smallest decisive next check is independent optical path measurement plus calibrated flight times. State uncertainty and outcome thresholds. Use section-specific geometry and a justified emission model for extensions. Add wall interactions when the observable needs them. Use independent clocks and appropriate sampling for a clock-rate test.
